Two decisions worth naming because they differ from the plan's sketch:

* **The trait takes a fetcher; an adapter cannot construct one.** `SafeFetcher` is
  the only way to the network, which is what makes §11.5's SSRF requirement
  structural rather than advisory.
* **The catalogue is registry-driven, not table-driven.** `sources` holds instance
  state (enabled, health, last checked); a source with no row must still appear in
  the catalogue, because a source nobody has imported from is still a source this
  build can read. The browser journey is what found this: on a fresh instance
  `GET /imports/sources` answered `{"items": []}` while a preview of the same
  address worked.

**The revision cache is a fetcher decorator.** `CachingFetcher` wraps
`SafeFetcher`, stores the body with the `ETag` and `Last-Modified` it arrived with,
and answers a second read from the cache when the source says `304`. The
validators are stored beside the bytes because they are validators and not secrets
(spec §10.4). Scope is the pseud's own row when a credential was used and the
public scope otherwise, so one reader's cached copy of a work they are signed in
for is not handed to another.

The eFiction adapter is this window's work and the milestone record's §7 has the
detail. Its findings in brief, each of which would have shipped as a silent
corruption:

* **The port's date table could not read either recorded member.** `%B %d, %Y`
  requires a comma and one member writes `January 18 2022`; the other writes
  `08/06/21`. Both returned no date, which becomes the epoch or the fetch time.
  Dates are now parsed by shape, day-first for the numeric form with the two
  readings disambiguated against the future.
* **A chapter's two URLs are the reading view and the *print* view, and a table of
  contents links to the print one.** `&chapter=K` links `printable.css` and fires
  `window.print()` on load. `ninelivesarchive.com` disallows
  `viewstory.php?action=printable&*` by name. The adapter reads
  `&textsize=0&chapter=K` and falls back to the other view's container.
* **A value is what the member rendered as a value.** Every class value is a
  `browse.php` link and everything else in a block is chrome — reading the chrome
  made a work's rating `Adult Download ePub`. Where values are links, the links are
  the values: that is what keeps `Male to Female, Young Adult (20-26 yrs)` whole and
  stops `Slow/Gradual Change` being split into `Slow`.
* **Archive statistics and story metadata look identical.** narutofic's
  content-gate page carries the archive's `Chapters:` and `Word count:`; read
  generically they become one work of 25,318 chapters and 47,323,633 words. The two
  are told apart structurally — container plus the `Completed:` label.
* **Three ways a page is not a story, and a fourth.** A moderation hold is a new
  `SourceError::Withheld` (terminal, and not the reader's fault); a content gate is
  `AuthRequired` and is satisfied by a credential's `adult_allowed`; a challenge
  wall is `Blocked`, matched on the interstitial's own markers; nothing at all is
  `NotFound`.
* **And one finding that was not about eFiction at all.** Every page in the family
  declares `charset=ISO-8859-1` and emits Windows-1252, and `SafeFetcher` decoded
  bodies with `String::from_utf8_lossy` — so `This week\u{fffd}s shows` was what a
  reader would have seen in their library, with nothing failing anywhere. Bodies
  are now decoded through `encoding_rs` by the declared charset, which is the
  WHATWG alias table every browser applies.

**Source reachability, measured** rather than assumed, by reading each member's
`robots.txt` and then requesting its work page: nine of eighteen are importable,
one permits a work page and forbids every chapter, two disallow the whole archive
(one of which is `tgstorytime.com`, whose markup is recorded in full), two answer
Cloudflare `403`, and four domains no longer resolve.

## The Cloudflare question, answered and built

The session's first stretch ended with §9 of `docs/plans/milestone-06-imports.md`
laying out four options and recommending one. The user's answer was *"I would
consider using flaresolverr, playwright or obscura, wayback machine, etc. rather
than not supporting it. so try and implement whatever you think would be best, or
multiple fallbacks"*. Both tiers are now built, and the deciding evidence came from
running things rather than from reading about them.

**TLS impersonation passes FanFiction.net, and keeps the guard.** Measured, not
reasoned: a plain `reqwest` client gets `403 "Just a moment..."`, and the *same*
request through `primp` with a Chrome fingerprint gets `200` and 46,808 bytes of a
real chapter. `primp` is a fork of `reqwest` that exposes `resolve_to_addrs`,
`redirect(Policy::none())`, `cookie_store`, `no_proxy` and `local_address`, so the
impersonating engine is built from the same vetted address list as the plain one —
it is a different socket under the same guard, not a way around it.

**What the wall actually is, and what the sources actually say.** Cloudflare's
challenge is bound to the TLS and HTTP/2 fingerprint of the connecting client,
which is why changing the `User-Agent` never helped. Read on the same day,
`fanfiction.net` and `fictionpress.com` publish `User-agent: * / Allow: /` with
`crawl-delay: 5` and reserve their `Disallow` for the AI-training crawlers;
`fimfiction.net` and `forums.spacebattles.com` are the same shape; `scribblehub.com`
challenges its own `robots.txt`, so its rules are unknown and it gets the default
pace. So the wall says *not from that client* while the site says *yes, at five
seconds* — the opposite of the `tgstorytime.com` case, where the archive says
`Disallow: /` and the import refuses.

**What was built**, in `crates/scrapers/src/`: `engine.rs` (the transport seam and
the challenge detector), `solver.rs` (the FlareSolverr v1 protocol, which Byparr and
obscura-solverr also speak, so the tool is a configuration value), `archive.rs` (an
Internet Archive snapshot as the last resort), and in `safety.rs` the `Unblock`
policy and the chain. The transport is a seam inside `SafeFetcher` rather than a
second fetcher, because pinning, hand-rolled redirects, the bounded read, the
`robots.txt` gate and the pacing are all transport-independent and a parallel
implementation would have drifted from them within a milestone.

**Three traps, each found by running it:**

1. **`challenge-platform` is not a wall marker.** It is the URL of Cloudflare's
   script and appears in the `<script>` tag of ordinary served pages — including
   every `fanfiction.net` chapter and every `royalroad.com` page. An earlier draft
   of the plan would have matched on it and marked successful reads as failures. The
   detector now matches on `cf-mitigated: challenge`, `server-timing: chlray`,
   `Just a moment`, `__cf_chl` and `Attention Required! | Cloudflare`, all read off
   real challenge responses, and a test holds the negative case.
2. **A fingerprint is only coherent if the headers agree with it.** The first
   implementation set our own `User-Agent` on every request and the wall refused it:
   a Chrome ClientHello announcing `Lorehaven/0.1.0 (+import)` agrees with nothing.
   The agent is now the fingerprint's, which means *a fingerprinted request does not
   identify itself as Lorehaven* — the real cost of the technique, and the reason it
   is declared per source rather than applied to anything that challenges us.
3. **Compression is the same class of problem.** `no_gzip()` is deliberately *not*
   set on the impersonating engine, because a browser sends
   `Accept-Encoding: gzip, br, zstd`. The bound is unaffected: the ceiling is
   applied to bytes as they arrive after decompression, which is the figure that
   matters for a decompression bomb. This corrects the opposite claim in §9's first
   draft.

**The live proof, and why it asserts the refusal first.**
`a_challenged_source_is_readable_through_a_browser_fingerprint` requires the plain
client to be refused *before* it checks that the browser client is served — because
a test that only asserted the success would pass on a day the wall was switched off
and would go on passing after the impersonation code broke. It also asserts that the
same fetcher still refuses a host the source never declared
(`169.254.169.254`, returning `Refused`). Result: `46808 bytes of the real chapter
page`.

**What is verified against a stub rather than a live service, and said so.** No
Byparr or obscura-solverr is running on this machine, so the solver tier is verified
against a stub over a real socket — the commands sent, the session created once and
reused, the session replaced when the service forgets it, and the refusal of a URL
outside the source's hosts. And `archive.org` answered *"Internet Archive services
are temporarily offline"* for the whole day, so the archive tier is verified the
same way: URL construction, provenance, the refusal of a snapshot pointing off the
archive, and a 500 that is HTML rather than JSON. What is left is an operator
pointing `imports.solver_url` at a solver and re-running the live suite.

**The live suite must run with `--test-threads=1`, and that was learned the hard
way.** Run in parallel they are never clean, and the victim varies:

| Test | Alone | In a parallel run |
|---|---|---|
| `archive_of_our_own_still_reads` | `ok` in 2.21s | once exceeded the 60s policy timeout and failed |
| `a_challenged_source_is_readable_through_a_browser_fingerprint` | `ok` 3/3 | once refused with `Blocked` — the challenge wall — while passing in another run |
| the whole suite, `--test-threads=1` | 8/8 in about 54s | (this is the only configuration that passes) |

A first reading of this blamed Cloudflare rate limiting alone, because the failure
seen first was the fingerprint test coming back challenged. A later parallel run
corrected that: the fingerprint test passed and an AO3 test that takes **2.21
seconds alone** ran past sixty. So the mechanism is contention rather than any one
test being fragile — the machine and the source's edge both see eight crawls where
they expect one, which is the impoliteness the pacing rules exist to prevent. A
failure here is a reason to re-run serially, not to edit a parser.

### Three bugs only a live service finds

1. **The configured endpoint was posted to as written.** `http://127.0.0.1:8191`
   answers `405 Method Not Allowed`: that address serves the service's
   documentation page, and the contract's endpoint is `/v1`. Every stub test passed
   while this was broken — a stub asserts on the *body* it is sent and never cared
   which path the body arrived at. The client now keeps a path an operator supplied
   and appends the versioned one to a bare address.

2. **Byparr has no session API at all.** The FlareSolverr v1 contract has one and
   the maintained successor dropped it: `POST /v1` takes a single
   `LinkRequest{cmd, url, maxTimeout, blockMedia, returnOnlyCookies}`, and
   `sessions`, `session` and `cmd` appear zero times in its source, over a browser
   it shares internally. The proactive `sessions.create` was parsed as a request
   for the empty URL and answered
   `502 Could not reach the target: … Invalid url: "https://"`. Reads worked and
   only the *log* was wrong — but an operator's solver log filled with errors about
   a service that is working, and every page paid for a wasted navigation. A
   session is now **proven, not assumed**, and a one-page fetch never tries: the
   first request goes stateless, because a session exists to make *later* requests
   cheaper, and support is attempted only once a second request makes it worth
   having, then remembered either way. On FlareSolverr proper the session is still
   used; on Byparr nothing is asked after the first refusal.

3. **The archive client followed a redirect it had not checked.** The real archive
   answers its entry URL with a `302` to the snapshot's own address, and `reqwest`
   was following that silently — a hop that could leave the archive, in a crate
   whose premise is that an answer is not authority to request wherever it points.
   Redirects are now followed by hand with every hop checked against the archive's
   host, as the source fetcher does, and provenance records the address actually
   read rather than the entry point that pointed at it.

All three were invisible to the stub suite, and all three are the kind of defect
that only appears when the other end is a program somebody else wrote.

### The archive tier, checked as far as it can be

archive.org is reachable again — it answered *"temporarily offline"* for the whole
of this session and now rate-limits this address with a `429` — which allowed the
URL construction to be checked against the real service even where no snapshot of
a given FFN page exists: a missing snapshot is a `404` and a present one a `302`;
entry URL and resolution cannot be told apart by shape, because the entry URL
already carries the `id_` modifier and still redirects; the bare-timestamp
fallback resolves to the **newest** snapshot (verified landing on `20260826131158`);
and `id_` is the difference between a page and a wrapper around it, measured rather
than asserted — **636 kB** wrapped against **93 kB** raw.

Whether a real snapshot of a real FFN chapter parses is still unverified: no page
of the recorded work has one, and the tier needs an adapter to reach. Everything up
to the body is verified.

## Milestone 7 — exports, device delivery and offline reading

Built in this session, on the operator's instruction to continue with the next
milestone.

**Themes, application state:** migration `0008` in both dialects (`export_jobs`,
`download_grants`, `device_deliveries`, `user_devices`), a rendering layer in the
domain crate that turns a work into bytes, the export service and its queue, the
HTTP surface, and a `lorehaven maintain` command that queues the recurring
sweeps for cron.

**The EPUB container is written here, not by a ZIP crate.** OCF requires the
`mimetype` entry to be first, stored, and with no extra field, and a
general-purpose writer treats those as options. The writer emits a fixed DOS
timestamp, so exporting an unchanged work twice produces the same bytes — which
is what lets the content-addressed blob store recognise the second export as the
first one's file. It is paired with a validator that re-reads the produced
container, and both were checked against readers that know nothing about this
code: python's `zipfile`, `unzip -t`, and python's XML parser. No `epubcheck` is
on this machine and that is recorded rather than glossed.

**Two of the three bugs in the service were found by the database, not by
reading.** The export row holds a foreign key to its queue row, so the job must
be written first; and my first fix — generate a `JobId`, hand it to `enqueue` —
was wrong because `enqueue` mints its own, so the key pointed at a row that was
never written. That is the more useful of the two lessons: the first fix looked
correct.

**An imported chapter is HTML and an authored chapter is a document**, and the
export layer carries both rather than pretending they are one shape. The HTML
side is a converter written against exactly the tag set the sanitizer permits — a
closed set — with a test asserting the two lists agree, so a tag added to the
sanitizer fails a test instead of silently losing a paragraph. The Markdown
writer is a small state machine because a block quote's marker and a list item's
first paragraph both depend on where a line *starts*, and neither can be fixed up
afterwards: my first version appended the quote marker when the quote closed and
produced `Quoted.` followed by two bare `>` lines.

**Retention is a promise with something behind it.** Seven days, a real
maintenance task, and a CLI that queues it — because a window nothing enqueues is
not a promise. The sweep *unreferences* the output blob instead of deleting it,
and the test proves that by holding a second reference and asserting the bytes
survive.

**The frontend half:** an exports page that offers the formats this instance
cannot make (with what to install), refuses until the privacy notice is ticked,
shows a queued export as a state rather than an apparent no-op, and lists the
server's exports and the browser's copies as two separate things. Offline
reading is a service worker that caches the shell and exactly one API response —
a chapter the reader has opened, network-first, never `no-store` — plus a bounded
IndexedDB store for exported files whose eviction rule is a pure function with
its own tests. Signing out *offers* to remove the saved files and clears the
chapter cache without asking, because one was chosen by the reader and the other
was not.

**Not built, tracked as `M7-03`:** send-to-Kindle and device-email delivery. Spec
§13.4 calls that adapter optional, this build has no mail transport at all, and
what ships is the schema and the refusal rather than a promise. The converter
formats are discovered at runtime and refused with an install hint when absent;
neither `calibre` nor `pandoc` was installed, so that conversion path was not
exercised end to end.

## Milestone 8 — the reader's library, closed in this session

M6 was finished first — `ffnet`, `wattpad`, `ficbook`, `scribblehub` and the three
XenForo boards, every one of them read live, `M6-02` closed — and then M8.

**The library is a place now.** Shelves, private tags, bookmarks with notes,
reading statuses, saved views, batch removal and storage accounting, in migration
0009 for both dialects. `docs/plans/milestone-08-library.md` is the record;
`docs/verification.md` carries the fourteen acceptance criteria with their
evidence.

Three things are worth repeating here because they were found rather than planned:

* **The batch summary was a sentence that stopped mid-clause.** `summary("removed")`
  produced `2 removed, 1 could not be` — one verb form cannot fill both positions.
  It takes the participle and the infinitive now, and names the total. The test
  that caught it asserts the sentence a *reader* is shown, which is why no
  count-based test had.
* **Removing an item left a dangling reference.** The first version kept the
  `content_references` row after deleting the library item, so the blob was held
  alive by a row that no longer existed and no sweep could reach. The references
  now always go and `delete_copy` decides when the *bytes* follow.
* **The migration parity test compared ids only.** That gap is what let
  PostgreSQL's `reading_progress` index lose `device_id`. It now compares tables,
  columns and index columns, and it was falsified before being trusted: removing a
  column from the PostgreSQL half makes it name the migration and the table.

Whole-work mode landed with it, paginating — the only way spec §9.2's "read on"
and its "never render every paragraph at once" both hold — and `remember()` now
follows the chapter on screen rather than the one the address names, so a reader
who reads on and leaves resumes where they stopped instead of at chapter one.

**Not verified, and said so:** the library tables have not been run against a live
PostgreSQL server, and the update check's per-item fetch has not been run against
a live source. The dialect rules were followed and the parity test covers declared
shape; those are different claims from "PostgreSQL accepted it".

## Spec additions folded in from the FicNexus comparison

Three ideas from reading the older tree were worth taking, and they are in
`docs/spec.md` rather than in a note, because a note is where an idea goes to be
lost:

* **§11.1 now states the transport rule.** An adapter is handed its fetcher for the
  call it makes and holds none — which the code has done since the first adapter,
  and which the crate documented while the spec did not. It is a requirement rather
  than a preference because the alternative fails quietly: an adapter that built its
  own client would compile, satisfy the trait and pass its parser tests while
  bypassing every §11.5 guard, and the bypass would sit inside a call that looks
  ordinary.
* **§11.14 Import result quality** is new. §11.8 grades the source; this grades the
  result, and conflating them is how a source that answers every request with a
  challenge page reads as `healthy`. Accepted, rejected with a reason, or held for a
  person — with a zero word count held rather than rejected, because a real work with
  no counted words exists. Specified, not built: `M6-13`, `unsupported`.
* **§23.11 Compatibility surfaces** fixes the shape for a surface that does not ship.
  The borrowable part was not "build one" but how one is built when it is wanted:
  its own prefix, provenance-marked, pinned by a test that reproduces the original
  algorithm rather than a comment claiming equivalence, and its incompatibilities
  documented as known rather than discovered. `M16-02`, `unsupported`.

`docs/requirements.csv` carries all three (`M6-13`, `M6-14`, `M16-02`) and
`docs/plans/milestone-06-carried-forward.md` §3 names the one that is a real gap.

**What was deliberately not taken:** FicNexus's XP/levels/rank gates, its
"107+ sites" shipped claim, its visible reputation score, and its legacy
`{"err": 0}` response envelope. The first three are §28.10 material and the fourth
is the reason §23.11 has a paragraph about envelopes.
